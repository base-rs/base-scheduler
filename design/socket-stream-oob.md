# Socket Stream Urgent-Data Policy

## Requirement

Socket stream readers default to OOB support disabled. Applications can select support at compile time with `reader.with_oob_support::<true>()`, without changing scheduler factories or introducing a runtime option branch in the specialized receive path.

Selecting either policy consumes the reader and preserves its scheduler binding, socket, registration, receive pool, and established-read, EOF and close state. It neither initiates I/O nor creates another task or receive queue. Cancellation and resource-retirement contracts remain unchanged.

Enabled support must preserve ordinary-byte progress across TCP urgent marks, including when a socket alias consumes the urgent byte. The option does not receive the urgent byte itself. Disabled support permits an `Unsupported` error for an observed urgent condition, and applications must not consume urgent data through aliases in that mode. Disabled support is not a network filter or a guarantee that every urgent byte is detected. Backends may retain a conservative implementation for both settings.

Every stream receive which consumes input follows one receive rule on each backend, whatever its shape: `read_into`, `read_owned`, `read_vectored`, `read_vectored_with_flags` and `read_into_with_flags` without `peek`, `read_owned_vectored` without `peek`, and pooled `read`. Once the reader has received data, it waits on cached readiness before its syscall. On native Linux epoll and io_uring, a disabled reader may clear its observed readiness after a positive short receive, one filling less than its total capacity, when no closure condition has been reported, and it rejects a reported urgent condition with `Unsupported`. It must preserve a newer notification racing that clear. Reported EOF and urgent conditions remain sticky across ordinary readiness clears. Enabled readers retain readiness until `WouldBlock` to preserve progress across urgent marks. Compatibility polling without directional EOF information retains conservative reads. On io_uring, mixed input/priority wake hints are advisory; only isolated priority notifications establish an urgent condition for a disabled reader. A pooled read whose stream would wait does so before taking a slot, and one whose receive would block releases its slot before waiting. On the readiness selector, owned receives complete synchronously within readiness on the reader's registration, so they follow the borrowed rule. On io_uring, owned receives submit `Recv` or `RecvMsg` to the ring, which consults no cached readiness.

A peek (`SocketRecvFlags::peek`) consumes nothing, so neither its count nor a wait proves anything about the queue. It is the one exception on every backend: it reaches the socket on its first poll, retains readiness until `WouldBlock`, and leaves urgent data to the next consuming receive. Any future flag which does not consume input must take the same path.

The Tokio adapter on Linux and Android applies the same short-read rule to disabled readers on a stream that has completed a read. Its registration requests priority readiness, and it keeps readiness instead of clearing while urgent data or read closure has been reported, so a disabled reader keeps ordinary-byte progress across an urgent mark rather than rejecting it, unless an alias consumed the urgent byte before Tokio observed it ([#107](https://github.com/base-rs/base-scheduler/issues/107)). Priority readiness is never cleared, so once a socket has reported urgent data its reads keep draining through `WouldBlock`. Tokio's readiness ticks preserve a newer notification racing the clear. Enabled readers, peeks and other platforms retain readiness until `WouldBlock`.

macOS retains its existing byte-count and EOF policy for both settings. A short consuming receive may clear its observed readiness only after consuming at least the event's reported available bytes and when EOF was not reported.

Because a consuming receive may wait first, polling it once can miss input already queued. `try_read_into` and `try_read_vectored` attempt one receive now ([immediate reads](stream-immediate-read.md)).

## Rationale

The HTTP performance investigation identified a redundant receive after ordinary short Linux TCP reads. The Tokio adapter later showed the same cost: with one worker, its borrowed echo trailed raw Tokio, whose streams clear after short reads, by about 18%, and issued a receive returning `WouldBlock` on every request. The user requested a compile-time OOB option, disabled by default, so the common scalar path can avoid that receive while applications needing urgent-mark progress can select conservative handling. Platform-specific event information limits where the optimization is valid.

The rule first covered only scalar borrowed and owned reads; vectored, flagged, owned vectored and pooled receives made a syscall first on every call, kept readiness until `WouldBlock` and never rejected urgent data, and the readiness selector carried a `SCALAR` parameter to keep that distinction. The maintainer decided on 7 November 2026 to apply the scalar rule to every non-peek stream receive on every backend, provided performance was the same or better, for interface consistency: a receive's readiness and urgent-data behavior should not depend on whether its storage is contiguous, owned or pooled, and a backend-specific special case needs a reason. A short consuming receive proves a drained queue whatever its shape; a peek proves nothing. 64-byte echo improved on every converted path with one worker, by 11–36% more requests per second and 14–33% less CPU per request, and by 2–16% and 6–21% with four workers, while scalar paths stayed within build-layout noise ([measurements](../benchmarks/echo/results/unified-receive/readme.md)).

## Scope

The public `SocketStreamReader` adapter and backend stream-reader implementations, including the Tokio adapter's, and conversion of live readers between policies. Packet and pipe receives and delivery of the TCP urgent byte are outside this requirement: packets have no stream queue whose drain a short receive proves, and pipes have no urgent data.

## Validation

Inspect the public adapter, readiness implementation, and platform event dispatch. For each consuming receive shape on each backend, exercise readiness-first waits once established, short-read clearing, full reads keeping readiness, EOF with final data, cancellation, close, and receive-pool retention; owned sequences must return on cancellation and pooled slots must be free while waiting. Exercise notification-generation races on the shared receive loop of each backend, and live-reader policy changes. Exercise peeks reaching the socket while readiness is clear and keeping readiness after a short peek. On Linux and macOS, exercise normal bytes before and after an urgent mark with enabled support, both with and without a socket alias consuming the urgent byte. On native Linux epoll, verify that an observed urgent condition rejects every disabled consuming receive, that a peek does not, and that selecting enabled support preserves the remaining normal bytes. On io_uring, exercise disabled short-read clearing with notification-generation races and sticky closure, rejection of isolated priority notifications by every disabled consuming receive, ordinary input with mixed priority hints, and conservative enabled reads across urgent marks. On the Tokio adapter on Linux, exercise short-read clearing under each policy for every receive shape, peeks keeping readiness, a notification racing the clear, and trailing bytes after an urgent mark with support disabled.

Compare optimized-build HTTP and raw socket workloads against a preserved baseline with matched worker counts, response bytes, connection reuse and task context. Record both throughput and process CPU per response, including low-concurrency tradeoffs. Performance measurements support the implementation choice; no numerical acceptance threshold is established by this requirement.
